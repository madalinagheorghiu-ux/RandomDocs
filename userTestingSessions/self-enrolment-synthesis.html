Self-enrolment links & solution access — synthesis

Three usability sessions on the solution access page and self-enrolment links: how access is described, how links are created and managed, whether people should arrive as individuals or in groups, and — in one session — the end-user journey after clicking a link. 54 individual findings, consolidated.

1Describing access

1.1The access labels

Two of three read them correctly. The third did not — and the session proved that renaming will not fix it.

Key points
  • Two of three understood the access states immediately, at solution level and on the access page. Alex GojCristi D
  • The third read them as how end users get into the platform, not as how the solution is configured — the confusion the team had already seen. Cătălin
  • Renaming was tested live, and failed. Offered "who can access" with "everyone" in place of "public", he landed in exactly the same place. The word is not the problem. Cătălin
  • One genuine ambiguity survives in all readings: the label does not say what signing in actually does — whether the account is created at that moment, or an existing account is assigned to a group. Cristi D
Strengths
  • Solution-level access read correctly at first glance, and the reading held when it was challenged. Alex Goj
  • The labels confused nobody who already knows the domain — not on a first visit to the solution, and not now. Cristi D
  • The page explains its own purpose from the title alone: who has access, and whether they got it directly or through a group. Cristi D
  • Even the participant who misread it produced two usable alternatives — "Public / Private", or "no authentication / with authentication" — offered for people less familiar with access, not for himself. Cătălin
Weak points
  • The Access column can read as container-app entry: public meaning you get in without logging in, Requires Sign In meaning you are redirected to a login and then checked by group. Cătălin
  • Relabelling is not the fix. A live rewording test moved his reading not at all — so the problem sits in placement, or in the concept the column is trying to express. Cătălin
  • What happens at sign-in is undefined: is the user created then, or assigned to a group then? Cristi D
  • The difficulty may be intrinsic rather than fixable by copy. Offering that much granularity necessarily means whoever grants access has to know a lot about the application — which makes it an audience question. Cristi D

1.2The access page, and getting around it

The core design decision — a dedicated page rather than stacked dialogs — was endorsed. The controls inside it were not.

Key points
  • The dedicated page is the right call, explicitly. It removes the dialog-over-dialog stack, and leaves one dialog with the page as context behind it. Cristi D
  • Two of three found the page without being told — one by clicking the row unprompted, one by muscle memory from where Share used to be. CătălinAlex Goj
  • Every complaint here is about a control, not the layout: the arrow that opens each row, the missing way back out of a nested dialog, and the policy details in the header. all three
Strengths
  • Clicking the row felt natural. The cursor changing was enough of a cue — he did not expect a button and did not need one. Cătălin
  • The whole page was read correctly on a first pass: the links, the group, the role controls, and what each row would open. CătălinCristi D
  • Asking for confirmation in place beats opening a dialog for it. A confirmation step is needed, and keeping the surrounding information visible is better than covering it. Cristi D
  • Regenerating needs no extra feedback: the updated date is enough, because it is an action you take and walk away from. Cristi D
  • The copy throughout was judged direct and the right length, neither too long nor too short. Cătălin
Weak points
  • The arrow at the end of each configuration row promises to expand it in place. On both rows it opens a separate window instead, and it caught the same person out twice. Alex Goj
  • There is no way back out of a nested window. Opening one link's list of joiners leaves only a close control, which reads as closing everything and returning to the start. Cătălin
  • The policy and build details above the access list are noise — and worse, they can mislead, because individual people get put on builds other than the one displayed. Alex Goj
  • Access has no named owner at the client. The product supports a dedicated administrator for roles and access, but in practice user management gets handed back to the FlowX team. Cristi D

2Self-enrolment links

2.1Creating and managing links

Creating a link is the most consistently praised thing in the round. What happens to links afterwards is where the disagreements are.

Key points
  • All three liked creating a link. One could not picture a simpler version; another called the experience genuinely enjoyable, unprompted. CătălinAlex GojCristi D
  • The first question anyone asked was about forwarding — what stops the link spreading beyond its intended audience. These are public sign-up links by design, but this release has no cap and no expiry. Cătălin
  • Revoking a link stops new joins but leaves everyone already in. That second half is missing and was asked for directly. Cristi D
  • What to do with spent links is genuinely contested — see the box below.
Strengths
  • The full lifecycle works: create, copy, refresh, revoke and delete were walked end to end with no friction. Alex Goj
  • Not being able to edit an issued link needs no fixing: a wrong role means deleting it and making another, accepted without persuading. Alex Goj
  • Dropping the approval step for the first release was accepted the moment it was explained. Alex Goj
  • Approval is also seen as the right answer to the forwarding worry — better than a domain restriction, because it puts the organisation in control of who gets in. Cătălin
  • Both gaps one participant found came with his own fix attached: a way to hide links that are switched off, and not opening anything at all when a solution has none. Alex Goj
Weak points
  • Revoking a link does not remove the people who joined through it. The campaign shape offered: a seven-day joining window, a thirty-day trial, then whoever did not convert is removed. Cristi D
  • Regenerate versus delete is not distinguishable: one participant could not tell them apart and had to ask what the difference is. Cătălin
  • The list of joined users is not useful without a sign-up date. The total is already visible, so exporting the names alone tells you nothing; with dates you can match sign-ups to a campaign. Cristi D
  • Dead links still offer actions that cannot work, Copy among them. Cristi D
  • Nothing bounds the openness of a link: no cap on sign-ups and no expiry in this release, both planned for later. Cătălin
  • Per-user enrolment has no stated limit: what happens at very large numbers, and at what point managing by group stops being optional. Cristi D
⚡ Unresolved — what happens to spent links? Hide them, or keep them?
Hide them behind a toggle. Revoked links stay in the list permanently and cannot be reactivated; ten of them means scrolling inside a dialog. — Alex Goj
Keep them, they are the campaign history. Someone will want to look back and see how many joined through each — comparing a campus flyer QR against a website call-to-action to see which performed better. — Cătălin
Keep them, for audit and marketing. He first questioned the dead Copy button, then accepted the case for the list itself and named both purposes. So the objection is to the inert actions on the row, not to the row. — Cristi D

2.2Groups versus individual users

The deepest finding of the round. All three want a group option; they differ entirely on how badly.

Key points
  • All three want the ability to put joiners into a group. Nobody defended per-user-only enrolment. all three
  • They sit on a spectrum, not a line. For one it is the only mechanism he has; for another it is what he would choose because it is cleaner; for the third it should be an option but never required. Alex GojCristi DCătălin
  • Group-on-link-creation was in the original design and deferred for reasons the team already knows — the empty system group, and the checks needed when adding to an existing group already bound to the solution with a different role.
  • The strongest single contribution is a cleanup rule keyed on provenance, arrived at unprompted — see strengths. Cristi D
Strengths
  • A precise cleanup rule, reached unprompted: a group created automatically by an enrolment link should disappear when the link and its users are gone — but a group that already existed and was merely pointed at must survive, because it can hold people from elsewhere. The deciding factor is who created it. Cristi D
  • The group preference is grounded in production, not tidiness: one Keycloak setup had five hundred users, and adding them to a list one at a time was unworkable by the ninth. Cristi D
  • Even the least group-oriented participant found a real use for them minutes after saying he would not necessarily want one: students sharing a role but split by year, where one group gets something extra and fourth-years lose access on graduation. Cătălin
  • Self-enrolment correctly has no place for designer accounts: designers are invited from the designer users area, and the client should not reach that side at all. Cătălin
Weak points
  • For one participant groups are the only mechanism, full stop. He does not grant access person by person at all — so an enrolment link with no group setting drops everyone who joins outside the only structure he manages access with, at accounts running to hundreds of users. Alex Goj
  • There is no organisation-level group to point a link at. Groups are built per business case and not reused across solutions, so a reusable target does not exist today. Alex Goj
  • Role versus group is still conceptually unclear to someone who has never configured one — he could not say when he would use which. Cătălin
  • Revoking a link and revoking access need to stay separate actions, with the group question deciding what each one means. Cristi D

3The end-user journey

Only one session covered what happens after someone clicks a link. Treat this section as one person's view, not the round's.

Key points
  • The registration flow produced the strongest single request of the round, raised three separate times. Cătălin
  • He talked himself out of two objections — entering an email twice, and self-enrolment for designers — which is worth recording as evidence those decisions hold. Cătălin
Strengths
  • One generic message for any dead link is right: the person clicking does not need to know why it died, and per-case wording would add little. Cătălin
  • Entering the email twice is acceptable on security grounds — he raised it, then reasoned it through himself. Cătălin
  • The approval and rejection screens read as fine, with simpler judged better. Cătălin
Weak points
  • Registration gives no sense of how far along you are. Raised three times. Merging the password step with the name step would remove the need for a progress indicator entirely — which makes combining them the better fix, if the underlying limitation allows. Cătălin
  • The login screen for existing users looks unrelated to the rest of the flow. He wanted the same split-page shape kept throughout, and described the current experience as jumping from design to design to design. Cătălin

4Decisions to make

  1. Spent links — hide or keep. Genuinely contested, and the two positions come from different jobs: one manages a crowded dialog, the other wants campaign history. A hide-by-default toggle satisfies both; removing the dead actions on a revoked row satisfies the third objection separately.
  2. Group on the link. Deferred, and all three want it. The open question is whether it is optional or required — the answer has to be optional, since one participant has never configured a group at all.
  3. What "Requires Sign In" means at the moment of signing in. Nobody could tell whether the account is created then or assigned to a group then. This is a copy question with a real answer behind it.
  4. Whether relabelling the access column is worth doing at all. A live rewording test moved nobody. If the confusion is worth fixing, the fix is placement or concept, not words.
  5. Bounding a link's openness. No cap and no expiry today; approval is the mechanism two participants reached for independently, and it was cut from the first release.
  6. Revoking joiners as well as links. Currently one action exists where two are wanted, and the group decision changes what each one would mean.

One thing worth not losing

The rename test is the most useful negative result in the round. Rewording the access column was tried on the one participant who had misread it, live, mid-session — and it moved him not at all. That is a cheap experiment that saved an expensive one, and it is the kind of thing that disappears from a report unless it is written down as a finding rather than as a moment in a transcript.
